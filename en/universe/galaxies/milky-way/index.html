    Galaxies

    The Milky Way

    Our galaxy: a barred spiral 100,000 light years across with hundreds of billions of stars and a black hole at its centre.

    On a dark night, far from lights, a faint whitish band can be seen crossing the sky. The Greeks called it galaxias kyklos, "milky circle", and legend said it was milk spilled by the goddess Hera. In Spain it was also known as the Way of St James, because it guided pilgrims to Compostela. That band is our galaxy seen from the inside.

    What our galaxy is like

    The Milky Way is a barred spiral galaxy: it has an elongated bar-shaped core and several arms winding around it. The disc is about 100,000 light years across but only around 1,000 thick, roughly the proportions of a CD. It holds between 100 and 400 billion stars and huge clouds of gas and dust, and it is wrapped in a spherical halo of dark matter that weighs far more than all the stars together.

    Where we are

    The Sun lies in a minor arm, the Orion Arm, about 26,000 light years from the centre, roughly halfway to the edge. It completes one lap of the galaxy every 230 million years, at about 830,000 km/h. The last time the Sun was at this point in its orbit, the first dinosaurs were appearing on Earth.

    The heart of the galaxy

    The centre of the Milky Way lies towards the constellation Sagittarius, hidden behind clouds of dust. There sits Sagittarius A*, a black hole of 4.3 million solar masses. Astronomers Reinhard Genzel and Andrea Ghez proved it exists by tracking for decades the orbits of stars that whirl around it at enormous speeds, and won the 2020 Nobel Prize.

    A 3D map

    Europe's Gaia satellite (2013–2025) measured the position, distance and motion of almost 2 billion stars, the most precise map of our galaxy ever made. Thanks to it we know that the Milky Way has swallowed smaller galaxies in the past, such as one called Gaia-Enceladus about 10 billion years ago, and that its disc is slightly warped like a hat brim.

    How to see it

    In summer, the brightest part of the Milky Way, towards the galactic centre, can be seen to the south after dusk, between the constellations Scorpius and Sagittarius. You need a sky free of light pollution. An estimated one third of humanity, and most Europeans, can no longer see it from home.

    Did you know?

    The Milky Way has two small satellite galaxies visible to the naked eye from the southern hemisphere: the Large and Small Magellanic Clouds, named after the navigator because his crew described them on the first voyage around the world.
