    Galaxies

    Types of galaxies

    Spiral, elliptical, lenticular and irregular: Hubble's classification and what a galaxy's shape tells us.

    In 1926 Edwin Hubble sorted galaxies by shape in a scheme that, because of how it looks, is known as the Hubble tuning fork. With some adjustments it is still used today.

    Spiral galaxies

    They have a flat disc with spiral arms where young, blue stars are born, a central bulge of old stars and a halo. The arms are not fixed structures but density waves moving through the disc, like a traffic jam that travels along even as the cars change. About two thirds of spirals have a central bar, like the Milky Way and the beautiful NGC 1300.

    Elliptical galaxies

    They are shaped like a sphere or a rugby ball, with no arms and hardly any gas. Their stars are old and reddish, moving in all directions. The largest galaxies in the universe are giant ellipticals, like M87 at the heart of the Virgo cluster, with trillions of stars and the famous black hole photographed in 2019. Many formed when several spirals merged.

    Lenticular and irregular

    • Lenticulars sit halfway: they have a disc but almost no arms or gas to form stars. The Sombrero galaxy looks a lot like one, with its huge bright bulge and its lane of dust.
    • Irregulars have no defined shape, often because another galaxy's gravity has distorted them. The Magellanic Clouds are an example.
    • Dwarfs are the most numerous: small, faint and with only a few million stars.

    Active galaxies and quasars

    In some galaxies the central black hole is devouring gas at a furious rate and its surroundings outshine all the galaxy's stars together. These are active galaxies. The most extreme are quasars, discovered in the 1960s as mysterious radio "stars". We now know they are the cores of very distant galaxies that can shine like trillions of suns.

    Shape tells the story

    A gas-rich spiral is still forming stars; an old red elliptical has nearly stopped. Astronomers use galaxies' shapes and colours to reconstruct how they grew and merged over 13 billion years. The James Webb telescope has found that in the young universe, orderly discs existed much earlier than expected.

    Did you know?

    In 2007 the Galaxy Zoo project invited anyone to classify galaxies from home. Within a year more than 150,000 volunteers had classified a million galaxies, and a Dutch schoolteacher, Hanny van Arkel, discovered a new kind of object: "Hanny's Voorwerp".
