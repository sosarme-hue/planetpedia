    Uranus

    The ice giant that rolls on its side: a turquoise planet with seasons of 42 years of day or night.

    Uranus was the first planet discovered with a telescope. It was found by the musician and astronomer William Herschel on 13 March 1781, from the garden of his house in Bath, England. At first he thought it was a comet. With his discovery, the known size of the solar system suddenly doubled.

    A planet on its side

    The strangest thing about Uranus is its tilt: its axis is turned 98 degrees, so the planet rolls around the Sun lying on its side. Most likely, a collision with an Earth-sized body knocked it over billions of years ago.

    The result is the most extreme seasons in the solar system. During its 84-year orbit, each pole spends 42 years in continuous daylight and another 42 in total darkness.

    An ice giant

    Uranus and Neptune make up the family of ice giants. Beneath an atmosphere of hydrogen, helium and methane, most of the planet is a hot, dense fluid of water, ammonia and methane. The methane in the atmosphere absorbs red light and gives it its blue-green colour.

    Uranus is the coldest planet in the solar system: temperatures of −224 °C have been measured in its atmosphere, lower than on Neptune, which is farther away. For reasons still not understood, it gives off almost no internal heat.

    Dark rings and moons from the theatre

    Uranus has 13 thin, dark rings, discovered in 1977 by watching them block the light of a star. It also has 29 known moons, named not after myths but after characters from Shakespeare and Alexander Pope: Titania, Oberon, Ariel, Umbriel, Puck and Miranda, among others.

    Miranda is one of the strangest moons in the solar system: it looks like a jigsaw of badly fitting pieces, with canyons up to 20 km deep. Its cliff Verona Rupes may be the tallest in the solar system.

    Just one visit

    Only one spacecraft has been close to Uranus: Voyager 2, which flew past in January 1986. In a few hours it discovered ten new moons and two rings. NASA considers a future mission to orbit Uranus and study its interior a top priority.

    Did you know?

    Herschel wanted to name the planet "Georgium Sidus", George's Star, in honour of King George III. For decades it was known in England as "the Georgian planet".
