    The fate of the universe

    Big Freeze, Big Rip or Big Crunch? How it could all end, a very, very long time from now.

    If the universe had a beginning, will it have an end? The answer depends mainly on dark energy, and since we do not yet know what it is, there are several possible scenarios. In all of them, the end lies an almost unimaginable time away.

    The Big Freeze (the most likely)

    If dark energy stays constant, as most data suggest, the universe will expand forever, ever faster. Distant galaxies will vanish from view, the gas to make stars will run out and stars will go out one by one. This is the Big Freeze or heat death: a cold, dark, almost empty universe.

    A timeline of the future

    In…What might happen
    1 billion yearsThe Sun shines so brightly it boils away Earth's oceans
    5 billion yearsThe Sun becomes a red giant
    ≈ 10 billion yearsPossible merger of the Milky Way and Andromeda
    150 billion yearsGalaxies beyond our group can no longer be seen
    100 trillion yearsThe last stars go out; only white dwarfs, neutron stars and black holes remain
    10¹⁰⁰ yearsThe last black holes evaporate through Hawking radiation

    The Big Rip

    If dark energy kept growing stronger, the expansion would become so violent that it would tear apart first galaxy clusters, then galaxies, solar systems, planets and finally atoms themselves. This is the Big Rip. Current data make it unlikely.

    The Big Crunch

    If dark energy weakened and eventually reversed, as some recent DESI results hint, the expansion could slow down and turn around. Everything would come back together in a Big Crunch, a kind of Big Bang in reverse. Some imagine it could give rise to a new universe, in an endless cycle.

    The vacuum bubble

    A more exotic possibility: that our universe's vacuum is not the most stable state possible. If somewhere a "bubble" of a more stable vacuum formed, it would expand at the speed of light, changing the laws of physics as it went. We would not see it coming. Calculations based on the mass of the Higgs boson suggest this is possible, but so improbable that it should not happen for vastly longer than the current age of the universe.

    Did you know?

    10¹⁰⁰ years is a one followed by a hundred zeros. If every second of the universe's current age were a whole universe, and every second of those another, and so on several more times, we would still not reach that figure.
