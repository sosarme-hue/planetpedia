    Caring for the planet: solutions

    Protected areas, species saved, agreements that work and what each of us can do: the good news from conservation.

    News about the environment tends to be bad, but not all of it is. The history of conservation is full of successes that show nature recovers when given a chance.

    Protected areas

    In 1872 the world's first national park, Yellowstone, was created in the United States. Today about 17% of the land and 8% of the ocean are protected in some way. In 2022, almost every country in the world agreed in Montreal on the 30x30 target: protecting 30% of land and sea by 2030.

    Spain has 16 national parks and about 27% of its territory within Europe's Natura 2000 network, one of the highest shares in the EU.

    Species we have saved

    • Whales: after centuries of hunting, the 1986 international moratorium allowed many populations to recover. Humpback whales have gone from a few thousand to more than 80,000.
    • The Iberian lynx: from fewer than 100 in 2002 to more than 2,000 in 2024 (see biodiversity).
    • The bearded vulture bred again in the Picos de Europa in 2020, more than 60 years after vanishing from there.
    • The giant panda was downgraded from "endangered" in 2016, and the mountain gorilla went from about 250 in the 1980s to more than 1,000.
    • The bald eagle, symbol of the United States, nearly disappeared because of the pesticide DDT; after it was banned in 1972, there are now more than 300,000.

    Rewilding

    Rewilding means letting nature take back ground, sometimes by bringing back key species. The most famous example: in 1995 wolves were reintroduced to Yellowstone; by controlling the elk, they let riverside vegetation recover, beavers returned and the whole ecosystem changed. In Europe, the European bison, the lynx and the beaver are returning to places they had vanished from centuries ago.

    Agreements that work

    • The Montreal Protocol (1987) saved the ozone layer.
    • The Paris Agreement (2015) commits almost every country to limiting warming to well below 2 °C. It is not yet enough, but before Paris about 4 °C of warming was expected by the end of the century, and current projections are between 2.5 and 3 °C.
    • The phase-out of leaded petrol, completed in 2021, prevents more than a million deaths a year.

    Adapting

    Even if emissions fall, some climate change is already unavoidable. Adapting means preparing for it: cities with more trees and shade to cope with heat, flood warning systems, drought-resistant crops, water management and coastal defences. The 2024 Valencia floods showed how important it is not to build in flood zones and to warn people in time.

    What you can do

    The biggest changes depend on governments and companies, but personal choices add up, especially when they spread:

    • Get around on foot, by bike or by public transport, and fly less.
    • Eat more plants and less red meat, and waste less food: a third of all food is thrown away.
    • Save energy at home and, if you can, choose renewable electricity or install solar panels.
    • Buy less and better: repair, reuse, buy second-hand.
    • Learn and talk about it: conversation is how big things change.
    Did you know?

    The ozone hole is proof that humanity can detect a global problem, agree and solve it. Scientists raised the alarm in 1985 and the treaty was signed only two years later.
