    Energy and renewables

    From coal to sun and wind: where the energy we use comes from, how renewables work and why Spain is a solar and wind power leader.

    Everything we do needs energy: getting around, cooking, keeping warm, making things, switching on a screen. Today about 80% of the energy humanity uses still comes from fossil fuels: coal, oil and natural gas. Burning them releases the CO₂ that drives climate change.

    Energy and electricity are not the same thing

    Electricity is only part, about a fifth, of all the energy we use. The rest is fuel for cars, lorries, ships, planes, heating and industry. That is why the transition has two steps: generate electricity from clean sources and electrify as much as possible (electric cars, heat pumps).

    Renewables

    • Solar photovoltaic: panels turn light into electricity. Their price has fallen by about 90% in a decade, and it is now the cheapest way to generate electricity in much of the world.
    • Wind: modern turbines are more than 200 metres tall, with blades longer than a football pitch. A single one supplies thousands of homes.
    • Hydroelectric: dams are the oldest renewable and the one that produces the most electricity worldwide.
    • Others: concentrated solar, geothermal, biomass and wave and tidal power.

    Spain, a renewable powerhouse

    Spain has plenty of sun and wind, and makes good use of them:

    • In 2024 renewables generated about 56% of the country's electricity, a record. Wind and solar were the top two sources.
    • On some spring days at midday, renewables cover all demand.
    • The Gemasolar plant near Seville was the world's first able to produce solar electricity 24 hours a day, storing heat in molten salt.
    • On the island of El Hierro, the Gorona del Viento plant combines wind and water and has supplied the island with renewables for weeks at a time.

    The blackout of 28 April 2025, which left the whole Iberian Peninsula without power for hours, sparked an intense debate on how to stabilise a grid with a high share of renewables. The answers include more storage (batteries, pumped hydro), more interconnections with Europe and voltage-control systems.

    The storage problem

    The sun does not shine at night and the wind does not always blow. So we need ways to store energy: lithium batteries, whose price has also plummeted; pumped hydro, which pumps water uphill to a reservoir when there is surplus power and runs it through turbines when power is short; and perhaps, in future, green hydrogen.

    What about nuclear?

    Nuclear power emits no CO₂ when generating electricity and runs day and night, but it produces radioactive waste that must be stored for thousands of years, and its plants are very expensive and slow to build. Countries are divided: France gets about two thirds of its electricity from it; Germany closed its last plants in 2023; and Spain plans to close its own between 2027 and 2035, although the timetable is under debate.

    Did you know?

    In one hour, the Earth receives more energy from the Sun than all of humanity uses in a year.
