    Population and human footprint

    From one billion in 1800 to more than eight billion today: how humanity is growing, what the ecological footprint is and why people speak of the Anthropocene.

    For almost all of history, humanity was small. Ten thousand years ago, when farming began, there were a few million people. Around 1800 we reached one billion. Since then, thanks to medicine, hygiene and more food, the population has grown eightfold.

    The UN expects the population to peak at around 10.3 billion in the mid-2080s and then start to decline slowly. In many countries, such as Spain, Italy, Japan and South Korea, fewer people are already being born than are dying.

    The planet of cities

    Since 2007, more than half of humanity has lived in cities. There are more than 30 megacities of over 10 million people, such as Tokyo, Delhi, Shanghai, São Paulo and Mexico City. Cities cover only 3% of the land but use about two thirds of the energy.

    The ecological footprint

    The ecological footprint measures how much of the Earth's productive surface (cropland, pasture, forest, sea) is needed to produce what we consume and absorb our waste. Today humanity uses resources as if it had 1.7 planets. If everyone lived like the average Spaniard, we would need about 2.5 Earths; like the average American, about 5.

    Every year Earth Overshoot Day is calculated: the date by which we have used up what the planet can regenerate in a whole year. In 2025 it fell on 24 July. From that day on, we live "on credit".

    The Anthropocene

    Many scientists say we have entered a new geological epoch, the Anthropocene ("the age of humans"), because our traces will remain in the rocks of the future: plastic, concrete, isotopes from nuclear tests, chicken bones. In 2024 the geological committee that had to approve it rejected it as an official epoch, but the term is widely used to describe our impact.

    Some numbers that explain it:

    • The mass of everything made by humans (buildings, roads, machines, plastic) overtook the mass of all living things on the planet in 2020.
    • 96% of the mass of mammals on Earth is humans and livestock; wild mammals make up just 4%.
    • 70% of the planet's birds are poultry, mostly chickens.
    Did you know?

    All humans standing shoulder to shoulder would fit into an area the size of the island of Mallorca. What takes up space is not us, but everything we need to live.
