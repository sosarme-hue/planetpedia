    Plastics and waste

    More than 400 million tonnes a year: where plastic comes from, where it ends up, garbage patches, microplastics and what works to reduce it.

    Plastic is an extraordinary material: cheap, light, strong and mouldable. That is why it is everywhere. But that very durability makes it a problem: a bottle can take hundreds of years to degrade, and it never disappears completely, but breaks into ever smaller pieces.

    A mountain that keeps on growing

    • In 1950 about 2 million tonnes of plastic were made a year. Today, more than 400 million.
    • About half goes into single-use products such as packaging and bags.
    • Only about 9% of all the plastic ever produced has been recycled. The rest has been burned, is in landfill or is in the environment.
    • Between 8 and 11 million tonnes reach the ocean every year: like emptying a rubbish truck into the sea every minute.

    Garbage "islands"

    Ocean currents form huge gyres that concentrate floating rubbish. The Great Pacific Garbage Patch, between Hawaii and California, covers an area roughly three times the size of Spain. It is not a solid island you could stand on, but a "soup" of fragments, fishing nets and microplastics.

    Microplastics: everywhere

    Microplastics are fragments smaller than 5 mm. They come from larger objects breaking down, from synthetic clothing fibres shed in the wash and from tyre wear. They have been found on the summit of Everest, in the Mariana Trench, in Arctic ice, in rain, in tap water, in table salt and in human blood and lungs. Their health effects are still being studied.

    Animals

    Turtles mistake bags for jellyfish; albatrosses feed their chicks coloured bottle caps; whales wash up with kilos of plastic in their stomachs. Abandoned fishing nets, "ghost nets", go on trapping animals for years.

    What works

    The order matters: reduce, reuse and recycle, in that order.

    • Since 2021 the European Union has banned single-use plastic straws, cutlery, plates and cotton buds.
    • In Spain, plastic shopping bags have been charged for since 2018, and their use fell by more than half.
    • Deposit return schemes (paying a few cents that you get back when you return the bottle) recover more than 90% of bottles in countries such as Germany and Norway. Spain is making them compulsory.
    • The circular economy means designing products from the start to be repaired, reused and recycled.

    How do I recycle?

    In Spain, the yellow bin is for plastic packaging, cans and cartons (not all plastic: a broken toy does not go there). The blue one is for paper and cardboard, the green one for glass and the brown one for organic waste, which is turned into compost.

    Did you know?

    A single wash of synthetic clothing can release hundreds of thousands of plastic microfibres. Washing cold, with full loads and less often greatly reduces the amount.
