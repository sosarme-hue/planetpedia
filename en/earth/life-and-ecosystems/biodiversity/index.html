    Life and ecosystems

    Biodiversity

    Millions of species, most still unnamed: what biodiversity is, where it is concentrated and why Spain is one of Europe's richest countries.

    Biodiversity is the variety of life in all its forms: genes, species and ecosystems. It is the result of 3.5 billion years of evolution, and the air we breathe, clean water, fertile soil, food and many medicines all depend on it.

    How many species are there?

    Scientists have described about 2.2 million species, and some 15,000 new ones are added every year. But the Earth is thought to hold about 8.7 million species of animals, plants and fungi, not counting bacteria, of which there could be billions of kinds. In other words, we know perhaps one in four.

    Beetles alone number some 400,000 species. When the biologist J. B. S. Haldane was asked what could be inferred about the Creator from studying nature, he is said to have replied: "An inordinate fondness for beetles."

    Where life is concentrated

    Biodiversity is not spread evenly: it increases towards the equator. A single hectare of Amazon rainforest can have more tree species than the whole of Europe. Scientists have identified 36 biodiversity hotspots, such as Madagascar, the tropical Andes and the Mediterranean basin, which cover only 2.5% of the land but hold half the world's endemic plants.

    Small but essential

    • Pollinators, mainly bees, are needed for about three quarters of the types of crops we eat.
    • Soil fungi form networks with the roots of almost all plants and help them get water and nutrients.
    • Earthworms turn over and fertilise the soil; Darwin devoted his last book to them.

    Astonishing migrations

    Each autumn the monarch butterfly travels up to 4,000 km from Canada to the forests of Mexico, a journey no single butterfly makes twice: several generations complete it. The Arctic tern flies from the Arctic to the Antarctic and back every year, about 70,000 km. And every spring millions of birds cross the Strait of Gibraltar, one of the world's great migration bottlenecks.

    Spain, a biodiversity treasure

    Spain is one of Europe's most biodiverse countries: thanks to its variety of climates and landscapes, and to having sheltered many species during the ice ages, it is home to more than half of the plant and animal species of the European Union, and the Canary Islands have hundreds of species found nowhere else.

    Its great symbol is the Iberian lynx, the world's most endangered cat at the start of the century: in 2002 fewer than 100 remained. Thanks to captive breeding and habitat protection, in 2024 their numbers passed 2,000 across Spain and Portugal, and the IUCN lowered its threat level. Other successes include the bearded vulture in the Pyrenees, the brown bear in the Cantabrian Mountains and the Spanish imperial eagle.

    Did you know?

    A handful of forest soil holds more living things than there are people on Earth: billions of bacteria, kilometres of fungal threads and thousands of tiny animals.
