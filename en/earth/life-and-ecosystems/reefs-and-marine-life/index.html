    Reefs and marine life

    Coral reefs, seagrass meadows and the giants of the sea: the life that fills the oceans and the threats it faces.

    The oceans are home to a huge share of the planet's life, from microscopic plankton to the largest animal that has ever lived. And within the sea there are ecosystems as rich as tropical rainforests.

    Coral reefs, the rainforests of the sea

    Corals look like stones or plants, but they are animals: colonies of tiny polyps related to jellyfish that build a limestone skeleton. Inside their tissues live microscopic algae that give them colour and food in exchange for shelter.

    Reefs cover less than 1% of the seabed but are home to around a quarter of all marine species. The Great Barrier Reef, off Australia, stretches about 2,300 km: it is the largest structure built by living things and can be seen from space.

    Bleaching

    When the water stays too warm for weeks, corals expel the algae living in them and turn white. If the heat does not let up, they die. Between 2023 and 2025 the largest bleaching event on record affected more than 80% of the world's reefs. Scientists warn that with 2 °C of warming the vast majority of tropical reefs could disappear.

    Mediterranean posidonia

    Posidonia oceanica is not a seaweed but a flowering plant that lives only in the Mediterranean. It forms underwater meadows that oxygenate the water, keep it clear, protect beaches from erosion and serve as a nursery for hundreds of species. The dead leaves that wash up on shore are not dirt: they protect the sand from storms.

    Between Ibiza and Formentera lies a posidonia meadow that, according to a 2006 study, could be a single organism up to 100,000 years old and about 8 km long, perhaps the oldest living thing on the planet. It is part of a World Heritage Site. Boat anchors are one of its main threats.

    Giants of the ocean

    • The blue whale is the largest animal that has ever existed: up to 30 metres and about 190 tonnes. Its heart weighs as much as a small car and its call can be heard hundreds of kilometres away. It feeds almost entirely on krill, crustaceans a few centimetres long.
    • Humpback whales sing songs that can last for hours and that change and spread from one population to another, like hit songs.
    • The whale shark, the largest fish, up to 18 metres long, is harmless: it feeds on plankton.
    • In the Strait of Gibraltar live groups of orcas that since 2020 have been interacting with sailing boats and sometimes breaking their rudders; scientists are still studying why.

    Plankton, the base of everything

    Plankton are the organisms, usually tiny, that drift with the currents. Phytoplankton photosynthesise and produce half the Earth's oxygen; zooplankton eat them and in turn feed fish, birds and whales. Without plankton, marine life would collapse.

    Threats

    Overfishing, plastic pollution, warming and ocean acidification threaten marine life. Marine reserves, where fishing is limited, show that the sea recovers if left alone: around the Medes Islands (Girona) and Cabrera (Balearics), groupers and sea bream are plentiful again.

    Did you know?

    The longest-lived animal known was an Icelandic clam nicknamed Ming: it lived 507 years. Scientists only learned its age after opening it to study it, which, sadly, killed it.
