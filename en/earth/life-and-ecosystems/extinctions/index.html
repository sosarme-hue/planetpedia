    The great extinctions

    Five times life came close to vanishing. The dinosaur-killing asteroid, the Great Dying and the sixth extinction now under way.

    More than 99% of all species that have ever lived on Earth are extinct. Most disappeared gradually, replaced by others. But five times in the last 500 million years life suffered mass extinctions: catastrophes in which more than half of all species vanished in a geologically short time.

    The Great Dying

    The worst of all came at the end of the Permian, 252 million years ago. Huge volcanic eruptions in Siberia, lasting hundreds of thousands of years, released so much carbon dioxide that the planet heated up and the oceans acidified and lost their oxygen. Around 90% of marine species and 70% of land species disappeared, including the last trilobites. It is the only mass extinction that also hit insects hard. Life took millions of years to recover.

    The dinosaur-killing asteroid

    66 million years ago an asteroid about 10 km across struck what is now Mexico's Yucatán Peninsula and gouged out the 180 km Chicxulub crater. The impact released energy equal to billions of atomic bombs, triggered megatsunamis and wildfires, and filled the atmosphere with dust and sulphur that blotted out the Sun for years. The dinosaurs (except birds), the ammonites and three quarters of all species disappeared.

    The hypothesis was put forward in 1980 by the physicist Luis Alvarez and his son, the geologist Walter Alvarez, after they found in rocks from that time, all over the world, a thin layer rich in iridium, a metal rare on Earth but abundant in asteroids. Some of the best exposures of that layer are at Caravaca de la Cruz (Murcia) and on the coast at Zumaia (Gipuzkoa).

    A sixth extinction?

    Many scientists believe we are entering a sixth mass extinction, this time caused by a single species: ours. Habitat destruction, hunting, pollution, invasive species and climate change are making species disappear between a hundred and a thousand times faster than the natural rate. According to the UN, around one million species are threatened.

    Some examples of species driven extinct by humans:

    • The dodo, a flightless bird from Mauritius, vanished around 1681, less than a century after sailors arrived.
    • North America's passenger pigeon, the most abundant bird in the world, numbering billions: the last one, Martha, died at Cincinnati Zoo in 1914.
    • The bucardo or Pyrenean ibex died out in 2000.

    The first de-extinction

    Before the last bucardo, Celia, died, Spanish scientists preserved cells from her skin. In 2003 they managed to clone her: a kid was born, the first extinct animal ever brought back to life. It lived only seven minutes because of a lung defect, but the experiment opened the debate on de-extinction. In 2025 a US company announced genetically modified wolves engineered to resemble the extinct dire wolf.

    Did you know?

    Woolly mammoths survived on tiny Wrangel Island in the Arctic until about 4,000 years ago: they were still alive when the Egyptians were building the pyramids.
