    Oceans and water

    Currents and tides

    Rivers within the sea that spread heat around the planet, and tides that rise and fall twice a day, pulled by the Moon.

    Seawater is never still. It moves in waves, pushed by the wind; it rises and falls with the tides, pulled by the Moon; and it circulates in huge currents that travel the whole planet and share heat between the equator and the poles.

    Surface currents

    Steady winds, together with the Earth's rotation, set huge whirls called gyres turning in each ocean. The most famous of their currents is the Gulf Stream, which starts in the Gulf of Mexico and crosses the Atlantic towards Europe. It carries more water than all the world's rivers combined and takes tropical heat northwards. Thanks to it, Ireland and Norway are far milder than Canada at the same latitude.

    The conveyor belt

    There is also a slower, deep circulation, driven by differences in temperature and salt. In the North Atlantic the water cools, becomes denser and sinks; it then travels south along the ocean floor, circles Antarctica and rises again in the Indian and Pacific Oceans. This is the ocean conveyor belt: a drop takes about 1,000 years to complete the journey. Scientists are watching whether melting in Greenland, which adds fresh water, is weakening it.

    El Niño and La Niña

    Every few years the waters of the equatorial Pacific warm more than usual off South America: this is El Niño, named by Peruvian fishermen because it appeared around Christmas. It brings drought to Australia and torrential rain to Peru, and pushes up the planet's average temperature. Its opposite, cooler phase is La Niña.

    Tides

    Twice a day the sea level rises and falls. The main culprit is the Moon: its gravity pulls on the water, forming a bulge on the side of the Earth facing it and another on the opposite side. As the Earth turns, each coast passes through both bulges every 24 hours and 50 minutes.

    • Spring tides: at new and full moon, the Sun and Moon line up and add their pull. Tides are bigger.
    • Neap tides: at first and last quarter, they pull at right angles and tides are smaller.

    The world's largest tides occur in the Bay of Fundy in Canada, with up to 16 metres between high and low water. At Mont Saint-Michel in France the sea comes in so fast that the abbey goes from being surrounded by sand to becoming an island. In the Mediterranean, by contrast, tides are barely noticeable: a few centimetres.

    Gibraltar, a gateway with two currents

    Because the Mediterranean loses a lot of water to evaporation, lighter Atlantic water flows in at the surface through the Strait of Gibraltar, while saltier, denser Mediterranean water flows out along the bottom. During the Second World War some submarines rode these currents to cross the strait with their engines off and avoid detection.

    Did you know?

    In 1992 a container holding 28,800 rubber ducks and turtles fell into the Pacific. For years they washed up on beaches in Alaska, Hawaii, South America and even Europe, and oceanographers used them to study currents. Some took more than ten years to arrive.
