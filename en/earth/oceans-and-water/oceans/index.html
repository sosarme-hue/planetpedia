    Oceans and water

    The oceans

    Five oceans that are really one: they cover 71% of the planet, produce half the oxygen we breathe and remain largely unexplored.

    The oceans hold 97% of the Earth's water and cover 71% of its surface. Although we give them five names, they really form a single, connected world ocean through which water circulates endlessly.

    The Pacific is so vast that all the continents put together would fit inside it with room to spare. The Southern Ocean was only officially recognised by many bodies in the 21st century. The Mediterranean, an almost enclosed sea, is renewed through the Strait of Gibraltar, just 14 km wide.

    The deepest place

    The deepest known point is the Challenger Deep, in the Mariana Trench, at about 10,935 metres. If Everest were placed there, its summit would still be more than 2 km below the surface. The pressure exceeds 1,000 atmospheres: like having an elephant standing on every postage stamp.

    In 1960 Jacques Piccard and Don Walsh descended in the bathyscaphe Trieste. It took them almost five hours to go down and they spent twenty minutes on the bottom. No one returned until 2012, when film director James Cameron went down alone. Since 2019 several expeditions have taken more people there.

    A world still to discover

    We have more detailed maps of the surface of Mars than of our own ocean floor. Only about a quarter of the seabed has been mapped in detail; the international Seabed 2030 project aims to finish the job. Most marine species are thought to be still unnamed.

    The blue lung

    Phytoplankton, microscopic algae living at the sea surface, produce around half the oxygen in the atmosphere. A single marine bacterium, Prochlorococcus, perhaps the most abundant photosynthetic organism on the planet, generates a large share on its own. The oceans have also absorbed around a quarter of the carbon dioxide we emit and more than 90% of the extra heat from climate change.

    Why is the sea salty?

    Rain slowly dissolves minerals from rocks and rivers carry them to the sea. There the water evaporates, but the salt stays behind. Over hundreds of millions of years it has built up to an average of 3.5%: about 35 grams per litre. If the oceans dried up, the salt would cover the continents in a layer more than 150 metres thick.

    Did you know?

    The ocean has underwater rivers, lakes and waterfalls. The largest waterfall on Earth lies beneath the Denmark Strait, between Greenland and Iceland: cold, dense water plunges about 3,500 metres with a flow far greater than all the world's rivers combined.
