    Oceans and water

    Rivers and lakes

    The Nile and the Amazon, Lake Baikal, the great waterfalls and Spain's rivers: the fresh water that shapes the continents.

    Rivers and lakes hold less than 1% of the Earth's fresh water, but it is the water we see, drink and use for irrigation. They have carved valleys and deltas and decided where civilisations were born: Egypt by the Nile, Mesopotamia between the Tigris and Euphrates, China by the Yellow River.

    Clash of giants: Nile or Amazon

    For a long time the Nile, at about 6,650 km, was said to be the world's longest river. But measuring a river is tricky: it depends on where you place its source. Some studies give the Amazon more than 6,900 km. What nobody disputes is that the Amazon has the greatest flow: it pours around a fifth of all the water the world's rivers carry to the sea into the Atlantic, more than the next seven combined. Its mouth is over 300 km wide, and not a single bridge crosses it along its whole length.

    Extraordinary lakes

    • The Caspian Sea, between Europe and Asia, is the world's largest lake: bigger than Germany. It is called a sea because its water is slightly salty.
    • Lake Baikal in Siberia is the deepest (1,642 m) and oldest (about 25 million years). It holds around a fifth of the liquid fresh water on the planet's surface and is home to seals found nowhere else.
    • Lake Titicaca, between Peru and Bolivia, at 3,812 metres, is the world's highest large navigable lake.

    Waterfalls

    Angel Falls in Venezuela is the world's highest waterfall: the water drops 979 metres, so far that much of it evaporates before reaching the bottom. The Iguazu Falls, between Argentina and Brazil, form a front almost 3 km long with some 275 drops. On seeing them, US First Lady Eleanor Roosevelt exclaimed: "Poor Niagara!"

    Lake Sanabria (Zamora) is the largest glacial lake in the peninsula, and Valencia's Albufera is a large coastal lagoon beside the sea.

    Did you know?

    Beneath the Amazon runs another "river": groundwater seeping under its basin flows slowly towards the Atlantic thousands of metres down. Its Brazilian discoverers named it Hamza in 2011, although many geologists dispute that it can be called a river.
