    Oceans and water

    Glaciers and ice

    Antarctica, Greenland and mountain glaciers hold almost 70% of the planet's fresh water. And they are changing fast.

    A glacier is a mass of ice formed from snow piled up and compacted over hundreds or thousands of years, so heavy that it slides slowly under its own weight, like a river in slow motion. Today ice covers about 10% of the land and holds close to 70% of the planet's fresh water.

    The two great ice sheets

    • Antarctica is covered by an ice sheet up to 4.8 km thick. It holds about 90% of the world's ice. If it melted completely, sea level would rise by about 58 metres.
    • Greenland has the second largest ice sheet: melting all of it would raise the sea by about 7 metres.

    Arctic ice, by contrast, is mostly sea ice floating on the ocean, a few metres thick. Melting it does not raise the sea level, just as a melting ice cube does not overflow a glass, but it does warm the planet, because the dark ocean absorbs the light the white ice used to reflect.

    Rivers of ice

    Glaciers advance anywhere from a few centimetres to tens of metres a day. Perito Moreno, in Argentine Patagonia, advances about two metres a day; its 60-metre-high front breaks off with great roars and drops blocks of ice into the lake. When a glacier reaches the sea, the chunks that break off become icebergs. We only see about a tenth of one: the rest lies under water.

    Glaciers are great sculptors: they gouge U-shaped valleys, carry huge boulders hundreds of kilometres and, as they retreat, leave lakes like those of the Pyrenees or Sanabria.

    An archive of the climate

    Each layer of snow that turns to ice traps tiny bubbles of air from its time. By drilling into Antarctic ice, scientists have extracted ice cores with air more than 800,000 years old. Thanks to them we know how carbon dioxide and temperature have changed through the ice ages, and that today's CO₂ levels are unprecedented in all that time.

    The ice ages

    Over the last 2.6 million years the Earth has alternated between glacial periods and warm spells like today's, in step with small changes in its orbit. At the last glacial maximum, about 20,000 years ago, ice covered northern Europe and North America, sea level was about 120 metres lower and you could walk from Asia to America. The Pyrenees, Sierra Nevada and the Cantabrian Mountains held large glaciers.

    Spain's glaciers

    Today only small glaciers remain in the Pyrenees, such as those on Aneto, Monte Perdido and the Maladeta, and they are disappearing fast: they have lost more than 80% of their area since the mid-19th century and several have already shrunk into mere ice patches. Scientists expect most to vanish in the coming decades. Europe's southernmost glacier, on the Veleta in Sierra Nevada, has already gone.

    Did you know?

    If all the Earth's water fitted into a one-and-a-half-litre bottle, the fresh water would be a little less than a shot glass, and the water in rivers and lakes just a few drops.
